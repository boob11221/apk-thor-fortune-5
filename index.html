How the peg pyramid works

The board is a triangle of offset pegs, with each row one peg wider than the one above it. A ball released from the top has a roughly even chance of going left or right at each peg, and that simple split repeats until it reaches the bottom row. The final landing point is one of the pockets arranged across the base — one more pocket than there are rows.

Because every drop is built from fifty-fifty turns, results gather near the centre and only occasionally reach the outer pockets. In probability terms, the board behaves like a Galton board, and the pattern it makes is a binomial distribution. The payout table mirrors that curve: the common middle pockets pay less, while the rare outer pockets pay more.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket multiplier is set in line with its probability, then trimmed slightly for the house margin. If you multiply the chance of each pocket by its payout, the total comes out a little below one; that gap is the operator's cut. Six words worth knowing

Pocket
The slot at the base of the board that holds the multiplier a finished ball lands on.
Rows
The number of peg lines crossed by the ball; more rows mean more pockets and tighter odds.
Risk level
A preset that stretches or compresses the payout table without changing the long-run return much.
House margin
The small difference between the true odds and the paid odds that supports the operator.
Provably fair
A system of published hashes that lets players confirm the result was set in advance.
Variance
The scale of swings around the average result — the part the risk setting really controls.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed sites, yes. Each deflection comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you verify afterwards that the path was fixed before the ball moved.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so the edge prizes increase while their probability falls. The overall return stays broadly the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

None is mathematically superior. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk concentrates value in rarer edge hits. Choose according to the swings your budget can handle.
